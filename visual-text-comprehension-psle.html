Visual Text Comprehension for PSLE English

How to read advertisements, posters, infographics and notices in PSLE English Paper 2. Covers a step-by-step approach, a breakdown of every question type, and fully worked examples.

📋 2025 SEAB syllabus aligned 🎯 5 MCQ questions · 5 marks

What Is Visual Text Comprehension?

Visual Text Comprehension appears in Booklet A of Paper 2. Students are shown a non-prose text (an advertisement, poster, infographic, or similar) and answer 5 multiple-choice questions about it. Each question is worth 1 mark, giving a total of 5 marks for this section.

Unlike the prose comprehension passages later in Paper 2, visual texts do not present information in flowing paragraphs. Details are scattered across headings, bullet points, captions, labels, images and different sections of a layout. Knowing where to look matters just as much as knowing how to read.

Why students lose easy marks here

Many students assume the Visual Text section is simple and rush through it. The most common mistakes are reading the question carelessly, choosing an answer from general impression rather than checking the text, and missing information tucked away in small print, captions or footnotes. These are marks that should be protected. The answers are always visible in the text.

Types of Visual Texts in PSLE

The PSLE can use any type of non-prose text. Here are the formats that appear most often:

Advertisements
Product or service ads, event promotions, campaign materials. Focus on the offer, target audience, and call to action.
Product adsEvent flyersSchool campaigns
Posters & Notices
Public health posters, community notices, school announcements. Pay attention to warnings, rules, and specific instructions.
Health postersSchool noticesSafety signs
Infographics
Statistics, processes, comparisons, or timelines presented visually. Data is often in charts, icons, or numbered steps.
StatisticsStep-by-step guidesComparison charts
Newsletters & Brochures
Multi-section documents with headings, columns, and varied content. Read each section separately before connecting them.
School newslettersTravel brochuresProgrammes
Schedules & Programmes
Event timetables, activity programmes, or itineraries. Questions often ask about specific times, venues, or conditions.
Event timetablesActivity schedulesCamp itineraries

How to Read a Visual Text

The difference between students who score full marks here and those who drop one or two is rarely about intelligence. It is about method. Follow these steps every time:

1

Identify the text type and purpose

Before reading any of the details, take 10–15 seconds to ask: what kind of text is this? Is it trying to inform, persuade, entertain, warn or invite? Once you know the purpose, the rest of the text makes a lot more sense.

2

Read every part of the text, including small print

Visual texts scatter information across headings, bullet points, captions, footnotes and small print. Answers are often hiding in the bits students skip. Read everything before you attempt a single question.

3

Work out who the text is aimed at

Ask yourself: who is this for? Children, parents, students, the general public? The target audience shapes how the text is written and why certain information is included. This clue often unlocks purpose questions.

4

Read each question carefully before answering

Visual Text questions are precise. They often point to a specific section, or use words like "only", "most", "except" or "not". Misreading the question is one of the most avoidable mistakes students make. If you are not sure what is being asked, read it again before looking at the options.

5

Return to the text. Never answer from memory.

Every correct answer is supported by something in the text. Do not choose an option just because it sounds reasonable or seems true in general. Find the specific part of the text that backs up your answer before you select it.

Common Question Types

The 5 Visual Text questions tend to cover a predictable range of skills. Knowing what each type looks like helps your child know exactly where to look:

Question Type What it tests Tip
Specific Detail Finding a fact stated directly in the text: a date, price, name, or condition Locate the relevant section before checking the options. Do not guess from the overall impression.
Purpose / Audience Why the text was created, or who it is aimed at Look at the overall message, the tone of the language, and any direct statements about who the text is for
Vocabulary in Context What a word or phrase means as it is used in this particular text Re-read the sentence it appears in. Choose the option that fits the context, not just the general dictionary meaning.
Inference Reading between the lines: what the text implies without saying so directly Your answer still needs to be grounded in clues from the text. Do not stretch beyond what the text actually allows.
Connecting Information Linking details from two different parts of the same visual text Read both sections carefully before choosing. These questions often have options that are partly correct but miss something.

Worked Example

Here is a sample visual text similar to what appears in Paper 2, followed by worked questions with explanations.

Greenleaf Community Centre

Eco Champions Workshop

A hands-on environmental programme for primary school students

Go Green. Think Smart. Make a Difference.

Join us for an exciting half-day workshop where students aged 9–12 learn practical ways to reduce waste, conserve energy, and protect Singapore's green spaces. Guided by trained facilitators, students will complete real hands-on activities rather than sit through talks.

What students will do:

  • Build a mini recycling sorter from upcycled materials
  • Take part in a nature trail with a certified nature guide
  • Participate in a group challenge: the Zero Waste Lunch
  • Receive a personalised Eco Champion Certificate

Important: Students must wear covered shoes and bring their own water bottle. Lunch will be provided as part of the Zero Waste Lunch activity. Parents are welcome to attend the closing ceremony at 1.00 pm.

DateSaturday, 8 March 2025
Time8.30 am – 1.30 pm
VenueGreenleaf CC Hall B
Fee$12 per student. Register by 22 Feb.

Worked Questions

Q1. What is the main purpose of this poster?
A
To teach students about Singapore's history
B
To raise money for environmental charities
C
To invite primary school students to join an environmental workshop
D
To inform parents about recycling rules in Singapore
Why C: The poster announces a workshop for students aged 9–12 and describes what they will do there. It is inviting participation. It is not raising funds, and it is not giving general information to the public. The language and content are clearly directed at students and their parents.
Q2. According to the poster, which of the following is NOT something students will do at the workshop?
A
Build something using recycled materials
B
Walk through a nature area with a guide
C
Listen to a talk by a government official
D
Take part in a Zero Waste Lunch challenge
Why C: The poster states directly that students will "complete real activities, not just listen to talks." None of the four listed activities mentions a talk or a government speaker. This is a direct detail question. The answer is sitting in the text; you just need to find it.
Q3. What does the word "facilitators" (paragraph 1) most likely mean in this poster?
A
Students who have attended the workshop before
B
Parents who volunteer to help on the day
C
Trained people who guide and lead the activities
D
Government officers who inspect the event
Why C: The sentence says students will be "guided by trained facilitators" and "complete real activities." The word "trained" tells us these are prepared professionals, and "guided by" tells us they are leading the activities. They are not participants themselves. Read the sentence carefully and the meaning becomes clear.
Q4. What can we infer about the Zero Waste Lunch activity?
A
Students need to bring their own packed lunch
B
It takes place before the nature trail
C
It is designed to teach students about reducing food waste
D
Only students who register early can join this activity
Why C: The whole workshop is about reducing waste. A "Zero Waste Lunch" in that context points to an activity about cutting food or packaging waste. The poster also tells us lunch is provided as part of this activity, so A is wrong. No order of activities is stated, so B is an assumption. And it is listed as a standard part of the workshop, not an exclusive offer, so D is wrong too.
Q5. A parent wants to attend part of the event. At what time should she arrive?
A
8.30 am
B
12.00 pm
C
1.00 pm
D
1.30 pm
Why C: The poster says "Parents are welcome to attend the closing ceremony at 1.00 pm." That detail is in the body text, not the footer. Students who only skim the times in the footer box would miss it completely. This is exactly why reading every part of the text matters.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Answering from general knowledge

The Visual Text is self-contained. If an option sounds true in the real world but nothing in the text actually says it, it is wrong. Every correct answer can be traced back to a specific part of the text.

Missing the "NOT" or "EXCEPT" in questions

Some questions ask which option is not stated, or which is the exception. Students who read quickly often pick a statement that is true rather than the one that does not appear. Get into the habit of underlining or circling these words before you look at the options.

Skipping small print and footnotes

At least one question in most Visual Text sets comes from the less obvious parts of the layout: a footer, a footnote, a small disclaimer, or a caption. If you only read the main heading and bullet points, you will walk right past the answer. Read the whole thing.

Choosing "partly right" answers

Wrong options are often written to sound convincing. They may be partially true but leave out one key detail. Before you commit to an answer, check it against the full question. An option that is 90% right is still wrong.

Quick revision checklist

  • Read the entire visual text before answering any question
  • Identify the text type and its main purpose
  • Circle "NOT," "EXCEPT," "only," and "most" in questions
  • Go back to the specific part of the text for each answer
  • Never choose an option that isn't supported by the text
  • Check small print, footnotes and captions. Answers often hide there.
